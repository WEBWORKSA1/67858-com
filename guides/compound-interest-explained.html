Compound Interest Explained: The Math That Builds (or Destroys) Wealth

By the 67858.com editorial teamUpdated October 27, 20267 min read

A plain-English guide to compound interest with worked numbers, the Rule of 72, and how it works against you on debt.

Compound interest means earning returns on your previous returns. It is the reason small, early savings can outgrow large, late ones — and the reason unpaid credit card balances snowball.

The formula

A = P × (1 + r/k)^(k × t)

P is the starting amount, r the annual rate, k the number of compounding periods per year and t the number of years.

Worked example

Invest 10,000 at 7% a year for 30 years:

  • Compounded annually: about 76,122.55
  • Compounded monthly: about 81,164.97

Now add 300 a month for the same 30 years. You deposit 108,000 of your own money on top of the original 10,000, and the account grows to roughly 447,000 — around three-quarters of the final balance is growth, not deposits.

Time beats rate

Doubling the time horizon usually matters more than squeezing an extra percent of return. Because growth is exponential, the final years add the most money. Starting ten years earlier can be worth more than doubling contributions later.

The Rule of 72

Divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate how many years it takes money to double. At 6% that is 12 years; at 9% about 8 years; at 24% (a typical credit card APR range) just 3 years. That last example is why compounding is dangerous on debt.

Compounding against you

Credit card interest is usually calculated daily or monthly on the outstanding balance. If your payment barely covers interest, the balance hardly moves. Run your own balance through the credit card payoff calculator to see how a small payment increase cuts months off the timeline.

Inflation and real returns

A 7% nominal return in a 3% inflation environment is roughly a 4% real return. For long-range planning, test both nominal and real scenarios with the inflation calculator.

Checklist

  1. Start early, even small.
  2. Automate monthly contributions.
  3. Increase contributions every year (a step-up).
  4. Clear high-interest debt before investing aggressively.
  5. Keep fees low — they compound too.
